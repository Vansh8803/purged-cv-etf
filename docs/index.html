Does leakage-safe cross-validation change what a backtest reports?

We trained one model on five ETFs, then scored that same model three different ways and measured how far apart the answers came out.

ES 335 · Vansh Dhameliya (25110337) · Aksh Hareshkumar Patel (25110017)
Code and every dated decision: github.com/Vansh8803/purged-cv-etf

What we found

Almost nothing — and the reason turned out to be more interesting than the result we expected. How much a careless backtest flatters itself depends on how you cut the folds, not on whether you purge. We cut ours as blocks of time, which leaks 0.33% of training rows, so purging had almost nothing to remove.

Shuffle the same folds and the leak jumps to 88%. You can try that two sections down.

1. What one row is, and what we asked of it

One row is one trading day for one ETF. It carries that day's prices, ten features computed from earlier days, and a label.

To build the label we draw two price levels around the day's close and ask which one the price touches first over the next ten trading days. If it reaches the upper level first we label the day 1; if it hits the lower level first, or drifts without reaching either, we label it 0. We also record the day it resolved, which we call t1.

We set the gap between those levels from recent volatility rather than using a fixed percentage, because a 2% move means something very different in a calm month than in a crash. Drag through the slider below and watch the barriers widen as 2008 arrives.

Two things show up straight away. The barriers are about 5% apart in calm 2007 and over 20% apart at the end of 2008, which is the point of scaling them. And most days resolve in a handful of bars, not ten — the label window is short, which matters for the next section.

A mistake we caught

We got this wrong first. Our original barriers were 2.5 times narrower. At that width a single day's trading range was usually already wider than the gap, so the median day resolved in one bar and the ten-day limit almost never mattered.

That would have wrecked the project. One-bar windows barely overlap, so there would have been nothing for purging to remove, and we would have reported our own parameter choice as a finding. Widening the barriers cost us sample size — 1,575 effective observations down to 662 — and we took that trade because the overlap is the thing we are studying.

2. Why ordinary cross-validation breaks here

The label for today is not a fact about today. It is a fact about a stretch of days that starts today and ends when the price finally touches something. If today takes six days to resolve and tomorrow also takes six, the two windows share five days of price history. They are not two independent observations.

So if today lands in the training set and tomorrow lands in the test set, we have trained on a row whose answer was decided by almost the same future prices as the row we are about to be tested on. The score comes out too high and nothing in the code looks broken.

Our 4,302 rows per ETF carry about 662 independent observations once we account for that overlap. Every error bar on this page follows from that number.

Purging is the fix: before training, drop any training row whose window overlaps the test fold. We also add an embargo, dropping rows that start in the few days just after a fold, because our features use 20-day rolling windows and a row just after a fold computes its features partly from bars inside it.

A training row leaks when a test row sits close to it in time. With blocks, test rows only sit near training rows at the eight fold edges, so the leak is small. Shuffle them and test rows are everywhere, so nearly every training row has one nearby.

Notice the two percentages differ. The picture above has 150 rows, and the real data has 4,302. The number of fold edges stays at eight either way, so with blocks the leak shrinks as data grows. With shuffling it does not. That is the whole mechanism.

We chose blocks for the control, for two reasons. Our specification says "standard 5-fold K-fold", and sklearn.KFold does not shuffle by default. And it is the harder test for our own claim: shuffling would have handed us a huge gap against a control nobody sensible would use.

3. Data and setup

We used SPY, QQQ, IWM, TLT and GLD — index funds rather than individual stocks, because a list of stocks silently excludes everything that went bankrupt. GLD launched in late 2004, so our range starts in 2005. We downloaded everything once in October 2026 and cached it, because Yahoo revises adjusted prices after the fact and a fresh download would quietly change numbers we had already computed.

5,282 raw daily bars per fund, no missing values, all five on the same NYSE calendar. Rows drop after the 200-day warm-up our longest moving average needs.

One quality problem mattered. Dividend-paying funds have an adjusted close that differs from the raw close, and our label depends on intraday highs and lows. Mixing raw intraday prices with a volatility threshold built from adjusted returns would let a scheduled dividend drop trigger a false stop-loss, so we scale the highs and lows by the same factor. GLD pays no dividend, so its factor is exactly 1.0 on every row, which gave us a free test of that code path.

The three schemes, and one metric baseline worth knowing

We scored every model under purged 5-fold with an embargo (our real number), standard 5-fold with no correction (the control, which is wrong for this data on purpose), and walk-forward, which only ever trains on the past. We also held back 2023–2025 entirely and opened it once at the end.

We report AUC, precision against the rate you would get by acting every day, and Sharpe with its standard error. One baseline needs saying out loud:

Read this before the results

An AUC of 0.544 means no signal, not a weak one. We generated fake price series with no predictability at all, ran the whole pipeline on them, and the full feature set still scored 0.544.

The cause is our own label. High volatility means wide barriers, wide barriers are less likely to be touched, an untouched barrier times out, and a timeout counts as 0. So trailing volatility predicts the label without any skill at predicting direction. Among days that did touch a barrier, 50.9% went up, which is a coin flip exactly as it should be.

Without that check we would have reported an AUC of 0.55 as a modest edge when it is the floor. Every AUC below is read against 0.544.

The same synthetic data told us how small a signal we could have detected: we start to pick up predictability at an autocorrelation of about 0.4. The real figure in these funds runs from −0.107 to −0.010, an order of magnitude smaller and the wrong sign. So when we find nothing, we can say we found no edge above roughly 0.4, rather than just shrugging.

4. Results

AUC by model and scheme, averaged over the five funds. Logistic regression is the only model clearly above the 0.544 floor. Both handwritten rules sit below it, which means a model knowing nothing but trailing volatility would beat our baseline.

The gap we went looking for — control minus purged — is effectively zero. Swept across every embargo from 0 to 20 bars, the largest value anywhere is about one thousandth of an AUC point, against a single fold's standard error of seventeen thousandths. Its direction even changes from fund to fund, so we cannot tell you its sign.

Control minus purged, in AUC × 1000. The two handwritten rules come out at exactly zero, as they must, since a rule is not fitted and cannot care which rows we withheld.

The best classifier turned out to be the worst strategy

Logistic regression ranks days better than anything else and makes the least money. Those are different questions: AUC asks whether the ordering is right, and Sharpe asks whether the days it chose to hold went up by more than the trading cost.

Pre-holdout, out of fold, no trading costs. matched is the Sharpe you would get holding the same number of days chosen at random, which separates how well a model chose from how often it was invested.

Most of the fall is not skill. Logistic is invested 22% of the time, and sitting in cash forfeits the market's drift whatever you pick — Sharpe scales roughly with the square root of exposure, so its ceiling was already 0.22 against buy-and-hold's 0.48. Then it picked slightly worse-than-random days, and then costs took the rest.

Errors cluster where the barriers are widest. The error rate climbs from about 0.48 in the calmest fifth of days to 0.64 in the most volatile fifth, and 2008 is the worst year for every model. The costliest individual mistakes are almost all October 2008, January 2009 and March 2020, at ordinary confidence between 0.52 and 0.70. The models were not unusually sure, just ordinarily sure and badly wrong. It is the same mechanism as the 0.544 floor: the conditions they fail in are the conditions their edge is built on.

6. The holdout, opened once

We fixed 2023–2025 in config.yaml before any modelling and did not look at it until everything above was finished. Then we fitted each model once, predicted those years, and wrote the numbers into our decision log before discussing them.

Holdout results, averaged over the five funds, no costs. Precision is read against the positive rate of 0.437.

The purged estimate held

Logistic scored 0.5755 under purged cross-validation before the holdout and 0.5754 on it. Every model held within 0.024 on three years nobody had looked at.

That is the strongest thing on this page. Whatever our pipeline was measuring, it measured it stably, which is exactly what we built it to establish.

Buy-and-hold still won, with its Sharpe rising from 0.480 to 1.116. That is the market and not skill — SPY returned 88% over those years and QQQ 138%. Every strategy roughly doubled for the same reason and none beat the index. Nothing reaches a t-statistic of 2, because 744 days gives a Sharpe standard error of 0.580 rather than 0.242.

Against us

Our one significant finding reversed. Before the holdout, logistic's selection edge was −0.164 with a t of −2.36, the only result we had that cleared two standard errors. We wrote that it was measurably picking bad days. On the holdout the same model's edge is +0.179.

Something that flips sign out of sample was noise, so that significance was a false positive — the exact failure this project is about. We found it in our own strongest claim, which is why we are telling you rather than dropping it.

The trees' holdout edge of +0.292 with a t of 3.4 is the best-looking number in that table and we are not claiming it. It is one figure among roughly sixty, from five funds, and it quadrupled from +0.071 before the holdout, which is what noise does. Its Deflated Sharpe Ratio is 0.860, short of any bar we would accept. That ratio exists to price exactly this kind of thing: it asks whether our best model beat what the luckiest of five skill-less attempts would have scored, and the answer is not clearly yes.

7. What we would not claim

  • Our 4,302 rows per fund carry about 662 independent observations. Most differences we could report are smaller than their own uncertainty, and that is a property of daily data rather than something better code would fix.
  • Barrier width is our most consequential single choice, and we fixed it before we had scored anything. Afterwards we measured what it cost us. Narrower barriers would have been worse, not better: at ±1σ the model scores 0.505 against a floor of 0.502, which is nothing at all. Wider would have scored slightly higher, at the price of more effective sample. We could not act on any of this even if we had wanted to, because the holdout was already open.
    Barrier width against what the model can actually find. The floor moves with the width, because wide barriers time out and trailing volatility predicts timeouts, so only the lift is comparable across rows.
  • Our label merges "went down" with "went nowhere", and only the second is predictable from volatility. That is where the 0.544 floor comes from.
  • The five funds share calendar dates, so rows overlap across funds as well as across time. We fitted one model per fund to keep purging one-dimensional, which sidesteps the problem rather than solving it.
  • Four of five folds train partly on the future, so the cross-validated equity curves measure generalisation rather than simulating trading. Walk-forward does not do this, and it agrees.
  • Our Sharpe standard error assumes independent returns. Daily returns are serially correlated and volatility clusters, so 0.242 understates it.
  • No embargo we could afford covers a 200-day moving average, so a little feature overlap survives at every fold edge.
  • The holdout is 744 days. It confirmed that our AUC estimate held, but it cannot resolve any difference between strategies.
